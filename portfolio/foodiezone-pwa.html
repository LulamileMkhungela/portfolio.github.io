FoodieZone is a smash-burger and wings kitchen in Pimville. Every order used to arrive by WhatsApp or Instagram, which falls apart the moment two people message at once. Customers order, repeat and track — the kitchen runs the menu from a phone.

FoodieZone is a hand-pressed burger and wings kitchen in Pimville, Soweto. The food was already famous — the problem was everything around it. Every order arrived as a WhatsApp message or an Instagram DM: items typed from memory, quantities guessed, addresses retyped, and nothing recorded once the food went out.

I designed and built the ordering experience end to end as an installable Progressive Web App. The live site opens on the bestseller — the Cheesey Smash Burger — then moves into the eight categories the kitchen actually sells: Burgers, Smash, Dagwoods, Wingzz, Loaded, Prego, Wraps and Chicken. Prices are real and current (Cheesey Smash R95, Death By Bacon R125, 5 Dunked Wings R55, Loaded Chips R45, Chicken Dagwood R75, Prego Roll R65) and the owner edits them without calling anyone.

This is the full process — discovery, menu architecture, flows, interface design, build, launch and what changed afterwards.

The problem

Every order started as a message.

02The menu only existed as screenshotsPrices and availability changed constantly, but the menu lived as images inside a chat history that nobody could search.ImpactCustomers ordered sold-out items and asked for prices that had already changed.
03Regulars re-typed the same order every weekThe best customers — the ones ordering the same two or three meals — did the most work, retyping a familiar order from memory.ImpactRepeat volume was capped by effort rather than by appetite.
04Nothing recorded what actually soldNo data existed on which items moved, at what time of day, or which categories carried the menu.ImpactStock, specials and staffing were decided on instinct alone.

Goals

Order in seconds. Reorder in a tap.

BUSINESS GOALS

What the business needed

  • Move ordering off direct messages into one structured flow the kitchen controls.
  • Make price and availability changes self-service for the owner.
  • Turn first-time buyers into repeat customers through an order history.
USER GOALS

What people needed

  • See the current menu and real prices in under a minute.
  • Reorder a usual without retyping anything.
  • Know exactly when the food will be ready.

Personas

PERSONA 01

The Regular

Orders the same two or three meals a week, usually on the way home, often on mobile data.

Jobs To Be Done

When I am hungry and short on time, I want to repeat last week's order in a couple of taps, so I can eat without a conversation.

PERSONA 02

The Owner

Runs the kitchen, takes the orders and cooks. Has no time to maintain a website and does not want an app store account.

Jobs To Be Done

When something sells out at 7pm, I want to mark it unavailable from my phone in seconds, so customers do not order what I cannot make.

Process

Seven days behind the phone, then the build.

5 stages — Discovery → Structure & flows → Interface design & testing → PWA build → Launch & iterate.

01 · Discovery

Seven days behind the phone

Before touching design I sat a full week of orders in front of me: which channel each one came in on, what was missing, where mistakes happened and what the owner retyped by hand every day. Then the customer side — how people decide what to order, and why half the baskets die halfway through.

02 · Structure & flows

A menu with muscles

The kitchen's own list became the structure: Burgers, Smash, Dagwoods, Wingzz, Loaded, Prego, Wraps, Chicken — eight real categories instead of one flat price list. Ordering shrank to menu, cart, checkout, confirmation; quantities step rather than get typed, and checkout asks only what the kitchen actually needs to cook and deliver.

03 · Interface design & testing

Food first, tested first

The interface leads with food: a hero shot of the smash burger wearing the bestseller badge, then the menu in order, the standard ("we don't do average"), and the call to order. Before a line of code, three of the owner's regular customers clicked through the order flow. Quantity editing in the order summary was unclear, so that screen was redesigned around it — fixed before build, not after launch.

04 · PWA build

Wired to keep working

Built as an installable PWA with a cached shell and menu, so it opens instantly and keeps working when the signal drops on the way to Pimville. Touch targets, contrast and status labels were built to the same rules the design was tested against, and the owner updates the menu from a phone without a developer.

05 · Launch & iterate

Feeding the funnel

Post-launch I tracked the funnel — category views, cart adds, checkouts, reorders — and reviewed it weekly. When repeat customers proved the strongest signal, the reorder path moved up to the home screen, and category order followed what actually sold instead of what had always been listed first.

Impact

The DMs moved into the app.

The PWA launched with the existing customer base migrated off DMs. Structured orders replaced the manual back-and-forth, and reorders — a single tap from order history — became a meaningful share of weekly volume. The kitchen now updates prices and marks items sold out from a phone in seconds instead of re-shooting screenshots.

Because it is a PWA there is nothing to install from a store: customers add it to the home screen from the browser, and the shell and menu are cached, so the menu still opens instantly on a weak connection. The Cheesey Smash Burger is pinned as the bestseller the way the kitchen talks about it, not the way a database sorts it.
